# Source record

This file is for us. It is not a page on lapinkyla.fi.

A sentence on the site names its source. A link is always allowed. A map image, a traced border, a photo, or a long quotation is not used until the row below says it is safe.

## Check the license, then we might show it

- The Swedish National Archives’ own image of the 1642 sheets. Most of their digitized records are marked public domain. Read the mark on this file before the picture goes on the site.
- Rovaniemi City Library, if we want their Finnish text itself rather than our summary. Ask, or keep linking.
- The 1928 book edition of the Tresk maps. The 1642 drawing is free. The editor’s essay is still in copyright through 2027.
- The Finnish Heritage Agency’s register of ancient sites, for dots from before the 1500s. Likely open data. Read the license first.

## Ask before using

- Kittilän Lapinkylä ry, before any photo, long quotation, or family material. The site stays separate from them.
- Rovaniemi City Library, before republishing their scan or their Finnish translation.
- Kaisa Korpijaakko-Labba, or the publisher, before any redrawing of the family tax-land maps. Citing the book needs no permission.
- The Swedish National Archives only if a file is not marked public domain and we want to show the picture.

## Do not copy

- Helmer Tegengren, *En utdöd lappkultur i Kemi lappmark* (1952), and his siida maps. Protected until 2045. The book may be cited. The maps may not be traced.
- T. I. Itkonen, *Suomen lappalaiset*, and its maps. Still in copyright.
- Siida outlines from Wikipedia. They are usually redrawn from those books.
- Anyone’s family tree.

## Read next, not on the site yet

- Veli-Pekka Lehtola, “Eteläisen Suomen muinaiset lappalaiset,” Muinaistutkija 4/2008. One side of the argument about southern place names. Read the article before any sentence about that fight.
- Ante Aikio, “An essay on Saami ethnolinguistic prehistory,” 2012. Free PDF from the Finno-Ugrian Society: https://sgr.fi/sust/sust266/sust266_aikio.pdf. It is about the spread of Proto-Sámi, not a verdict on the name Lapinkylä. Read it before using it.
- T. I. Itkonen, *Suomen lappalaiset vuoteen 1945*, vol. 1 (1948). Still in copyright. Wikipedia’s village table rests on this book and gives no page numbers. Use it only after a library copy has been read page by page. Do not scan it or copy its maps.
- Do not trace the four Ximfel1 drawings into polygons. They are on `/siida` only as unchanged images. The Commons pages call them the artist’s own work. If a line is in fact copied from Itkonen’s printed map, that book is still in copyright and the Commons licence would not clear it. Treat the drawings as illustrations, not as a source.

Sentences on the page, and where they come from:

- Schefferus 1674, the Oxford page already on the site. Summer and winter clothes differed. The winter coat was reindeer skin.
- [Sámi Parliament of Finland](https://samediggi.fi/en/about-us/). The five dresses: Enontekiö, Inari, Utsjoki, Vuotso, Skolt.

## The name, `/nimi`

| File | Record | Licence |
|---|---|---|
| `public/nimi/schefferus-1674.jpg` | Oxford page 2. He prints the self-names Sabmieladtz and Sameednam, from Sabmi or Same, and says the name Lapp was resented. His own explanation, that the people were originally Finlanders and that Lapp meant a banished person, is labelled as his opinion. | Public domain. |

The division follows Anders Bure in the book: the south and the inland to Sweden, the coast called Findmark to Norway, and the part from the castle the book calls Warhuis to the mouth of the White Sea to Russia. Under Sweden he first names six lappmarks, then counts Ångermanland with Ume. The five he treats apart are Ume, Pite, Lule, Torne, and Kemi. He names Jochmoch in Lule and Kithilaby in Torne. The Swedes’ word in the book for a family is rakar, and for the smaller part, by. The full list of byar is not printed here: the book says Torne has eight, and a reading of the line gives one name more than eight. No border is drawn. Kithilaby is the book’s spelling, not a proof of the 1642 village.

## Food, `/ruoka`

No plate in the diet chapter, Oxford pages 91–93. Mountain food is the reindeer, including blood boiled in water, and a few sheep, goats, and oxen bought from Norway. Wood food is fish, birds, and game, with bear preferred. Bread and salt are almost unknown. Strawberries are kept in birch bark. The drink is water, the broth the book calls Læbma, and whey. He prints two graces, Pite and Torne, and an English gloss. The Torne grace is not retyped. The empty frame asks Siida for a birch-bark vessel.

## Hunting, `/metsastys`

No plate was used. Oxford pages 94–98. Hunting is men’s work in the book, and he rejects Olaus Magnus on women hunting. Summer on foot with dogs, winter on skis. The bow is birch and pine, about three ells, covered with birch bark, glued with perch skin. Ermines in wooden traps. The bear passage is shortened to the drum, the shared meat, the skin hung for the women to shoot at blindfolded, and a wire cross for each bear. The songs are not quoted. The empty frame asks Norsk Folkemuseum for a bow.

## Games, `/leikit`

No plate. Oxford pages 107–109. A leap for distance, a leap for height, bow and arrow, a hay-stuffed ball, a bandy game on the snow, wrestling by a girdle he says goes six times round, cards, and wooden dice with an X on one side. The idleness passage is not used. The empty frame asks Norsk Folkemuseum for a wooden die.

## Marriage, `/haat`

| File | Record | Licence |
|---|---|---|

Oxford pages 110–119. Reindeer given to a child as a portion. The suit put to the parents. A kiss in which the noses touch. Gifts thrown down if refused. Songs not retyped. Church wedding. ## The reindeer, `/poro`

| File | Record | Licence |
|---|---|---|
| `public/poro/schefferus-1674.jpg` | Oxford page 130. The reindeer he says is drawn to the life, against the four horns in Lomenius. | Public domain. |

Oxford pages 129–132. Sheep, goats, and oxen bought for one summer. Wild, tame, and a third kind for the sledge. Rut about St Matthew, calving in May, one calf, full grown in four years, gelded at a year. A hundred does to twenty bucks. Milked kneeling, once a day. Cheese of ten reindeer, no butter. White moss under the snow. Ear mark, not the horn. The book’s ten years is the book’s figure. The Sámi names in the chapter are too broken in the print to retype.

## Tax, `/vero`

No plate. English edition, the chapters on secular affairs and tribute. The Birkarlar under Ladulaus, about 1277. The snow story is the book’s account, from Ericus and Bure, not a conclusion. Three courts from the instruction to Laurentius Laurentii, Stockholm, 10 October 1610. Tribute in skins, then reindeer and fish, then patacoons. Koutokeine, Aujouara, Teno, Utziocki, and Enare are the book’s spellings for the dues paid to Denmark and Muscovy. They are not drawn as a border.

## Fairs, `/markkinat`

No plate. English edition, the chapter on trade. Silent exchange, then the patacoon. Rheen’s list of skins, cheese, cloth, copper, and the metal he calls alchymy. The book’s word “cheats” stays the book’s. Charles IX in 1602 left the places to the governors. Christina’s edict of 1640 names Arfwisjerf in January and Arieplog in February. The shoe, glove, and salt rates that break off in the print are not guessed.

## Children, `/lapset`

No plate. English edition, the chapter on child-bearing. Birth in the hut, a hare skin, baptism often only in winter, the hollowed cradle, and a reindeer calf at the first tooth, called Waddom when a second is added. The moon rite is not set out. Olaus Magnus’s back-baskets are rejected and not shown. The empty frame asks Siida for a cradle.

## Burial, `/hautaus`

No plate. English edition, the chapter on death. Illness in a few sentences, not as a method. The drum is not repeated. The grave, the sledge left there, the feast on the third day, and Saligawiin. Talk of spirits stays off the page. The richer sort repeated the feast yearly. That sentence stops before the book’s reason.

## The school, `/koulu`

No plate. English edition, the chapter on the churches and the schools. Charles IX began the churches. The book names Tenotekis and Jukasjærff, Jochmoch in Lule, and Enare in Kemi. One church is spelt both Rounala and Kounala, built by three brothers. The rest of the list of thirteen is not copied, because the spellings change inside the paragraph. Gustavus Adolphus, a primer and a service book before 1619, Nicolaus Andreæ, then Tornæus. The Ume school by the church at Lyksala, royal letter at old Stetin in Pomerland, 20 June 1631, kept from the Ume tithe, in the care of Joannes Skytte, spelt John Skytt in the letter, and his family. The empty frame asks the Sámi Parliament for a primer.

## The year, `/vuosi`

No plate. English edition, the chapter on the air and the soil. Herbersten’s forty days is not the whole country. Petrus Claudius on moonlight and fishing. Nieuren at the church the book calls Thor, 24 June 1616. The snowstorm, the cave, two reindeer against a cart and ten horses, ice more than three or four cubits, and the mountains the book calls Doffrini. His closing claim that old Finland and Lapland differ only in name is not repeated. It belongs with the opinion already labelled on the name page.

## Metals, `/metallit`

No plate. English edition, the chapter on metals. Olaus Magnus and the boats without nails.
